Before a sorting warehouse starts its day, two questions need answers: roughly how many parcels are coming, and when the floor can finish. Those answers used to live scattered across the upstream platform's admin pages and the floor managers' experience. The numbers were visible; decisions were not. I built this warehouse an operations dashboard that puts the day's target, progress, productivity, and anomalies on one screen, and the floor uses it daily to plan the wind-down and the handover.

Building it, I set one rule: any panel that only displays information and cannot support a decision gets cut. Cumulative totals hide a gap of a few hundred parcels inside a base of tens of thousands, so primary metrics are rates (parcels per hour) and deviations against a baseline, with the most actionable outlier called out and pinned to the top. Every panel has to answer the same question: what decision can a manager make from this? No answer, redo it.

The engineering stays deliberately plain: a cloud collector pulls upstream data on a schedule, a single-file SQLite database, FastAPI plus framework-free vanilla JS, zero-downtime deploys. About 7,000 lines of Python across collection, forecasting, labor settlement, quality checks, and reconciliation audits, with the project still under active iteration.

Many metrics on the board anchor to the day's target volume, and its accuracy decides how much the whole board can be trusted. How that number gets computed is the most fully written-up piece of engineering in the project. What follows is the forecasting module's complete write-up, also published separately as a blog post.

Summary

In 2026, when the conversation turns to forecasting, the default opening move is a large model. This article goes the other way with a real case: I built a daily volume forecast for a cross-border e-commerce sorting warehouse using an exponentially weighted moving average (EWMA) plus day-of-week bias calibration. The core logic is about 30 lines of pure Python, with no machine learning framework anywhere. It beat every baseline and keeps the warehouse's actual finish time within half an hour of what the board predicts. The point I want to make with it: how advanced a tool is and how well it solves a problem are two different things, and in real operations with small data and a strong cycle, the traditional method is still the correct answer today.

The setting itself is simple. Before the warehouse starts each day, it needs to know roughly how many parcels are coming. The operations dashboard uses that number as the day's target; the progress bar and the on-time estimate both anchor to it. Previously the number came from one of two places: the platform's daily estimate, which runs consistently low, or a floor manager's gut feel, which cannot be audited afterwards. I backtested against 79 days of real data: the forecast misses the actual by 7.5% on average, taking the platform estimate as-is misses by 12.3%, and ignoring the platform entirely to extrapolate from history alone misses by 9.5%. Because the data comes from live operations, I withhold the warehouse and platform names, the chart axes are scaled down by a single common factor to hide absolute volumes, and every percentage in the text is real.

Business context

This warehouse sorts, bags, and ships parcels every day. The dashboard supervises the operation, and the daily target volume is the baseline for many of its metrics. If the target is off, the progress percentage and the projected finish time drift with it.

Before the forecast existed, the number had two sources, each with its own problem. One was the platform's daily estimate. The platform projects today's inbound volume from what the upstream stage has already collected, so there is real information in it, but it runs low across the board: taken as-is it misses by 12.3% on average, and on the worst weekday it runs almost thirty percent under. The other source was a manual entry from the floor manager. That relies on personal experience, cannot be checked afterwards, and often nobody fills it in at all, leaving the target blank.

So the thing I had to build had three requirements: forecast more accurately than copying the platform's number; make every day's forecast auditable after the fact; and fill the dashboard automatically while letting managers override it, with the page stating plainly whether the current number came from the system or from a person.

Method

Start with the shape of the data. Volume at this warehouse runs on a weekly cycle. Monday is the peak of the week, Saturday is the trough, and the gap is more than fourfold:

FIG.01: Average daily volume by weekday. Monday peaks, Saturday bottoms out, peak-to-trough 4.5x.

Mondays resemble each other closely across the history, but a Monday and the following Tuesday can differ by a factor of three. So there is no "average of the last N days" anywhere in the model. All history is grouped by day of week first, and a Monday forecast only ever consults past Mondays.

The platform estimate's bias, split by day of week, also turns out to be a stable pattern:

FIG.02: Daily (actual − estimate) ÷ estimate, averaged within each weekday group, 10 to 12 samples per group.

Tuesday's estimate runs 29% low on average; Thursday through Saturday run only about 5% low. This chart settled how to treat the platform estimate: it cannot be copied, but it should not be thrown away either, because the platform knows how much the upstream actually collected today, which history alone cannot see. And since the shortfall is stable within each weekday group, stable means estimable, and estimable means correctable. The right treatment is calibration.

The forecast formula has two branches. Most days use addition: forecast = platform estimate + the weighted average of recent same-weekday (actual − estimate) gaps. Saturday alone uses multiplication: forecast = platform estimate × the weighted average of recent same-weekday (actual ÷ estimate) ratios. Sunday is not special and takes the additive path like any weekday. Saturday gets its own treatment because its volume is only a third of a weekday's, and an absolute gap of a few hundred parcels swings wildly on a small base while the ratio stays steady; more on that when I get to parameters. When the platform estimate is missing for the day, there is a third path: a weighted average of that weekday's historical actuals, which is pure extrapolation. Days with data gaps and future days both fall into this case, because the platform does not publish estimates in advance.

The "weighted average" that keeps appearing above is EWMA, and this is the entire implementation:

def _ewma(vals, alpha=0.7):
    """vals in ascending time order (same weekday, one week apart). Newer values weigh more."""
    e = float(vals[0])
    for v in vals[1:]:
        e = alpha * float(v) + (1 - alpha) * e
    return e

FIG.04: All of EWMA. α=0.7 means the most recent observation (the same weekday last week) carries seventy percent of the weight, with the remaining thirty percent spread over older history, decaying week by week.

The decay rate is worth seeing drawn out:

FIG.05: The weight distribution at α=0.7. Last week carries seventy percent, the last two weeks together carry over ninety, and anything older than a month has almost no voice left.

The parameters were not pulled from thin air. The α=0.7 and same-weekday grouping structure won a design-stage comparison, run with the same day-by-day backtest, against two candidates: an equal-weight same-weekday mean (no decay, old and new treated alike) came out more than five points worse, and a seasonal-factor decomposition model came out two points worse. That comparison ran in a multi-day-ahead setting, which is harder than forecasting tomorrow, so its error levels are higher overall; I only take the relative ranking from it and do not compare those numbers against the 9.5% here. EWMA wins on responsiveness: peak-season ramps and channel shifts change the overall level, a model with its weight pressed onto recent weeks catches up in two or three weeks, and an equal-weight mean drags for a month or two.

Validation

The validation has one rule: each day's forecast may only use data from before that day, walked from the start of the timeline to the end. This is equivalent to asking "if this system had been live from day one, what number would it have produced each day," which is a walk-forward backtest. The opposite approach, tuning parameters on the full history first and then reporting an error on that same history, is an exam taken after reading the answer key, and it does not count. There are 109 days of history in total; the earliest stretch goes to accumulating samples, and the last 79 days can be formally evaluated day by day.

Over the same set of days, I ran all three approaches end to end:

FIG.06: Mean error of the three approaches over the same backtest days.

Two things fall out of these three numbers side by side. 7.5% beats 12.3%, and the gap is the systematic bias that calibration removed. 7.5% also beats 9.5%, and that gap is the information inside the platform estimate that history alone cannot reach. Both contributions are real, which says the platform's number is worth keeping, but only after calibration.

Whether 7.5% is good or bad has to be judged inside the business. The number's direct use is estimating when today's work will finish. Converted through the warehouse's end-of-day throughput, a 7.5% error corresponds to a finish-time deviation inside half an hour: the predicted end of shift is the actual end of shift, and cleanup and handover can be scheduled ahead of time. On the floor, that is a qualitative difference. Under the same conversion, copying the platform's 12.3% corresponds to nearly an hour, at which point the target is only a reference; within half an hour, the target is a number you can act on directly. For this business, 7.5% is a very good result.

FIG.07: The day-by-day backtest, with the two biggest holiday misses annotated on the chart.

The bad days deserve an accounting too. May 25 was Memorial Day in the US; upstream intake slowed sharply for the holiday, the actual came in thirty percent below a normal Monday, and the model, which knows weekdays but not calendars, overshot by 28%. July 6 was the make-up surge after the Independence Day break, the highest single day in the whole window, and the model undershot by 13%. Both mistakes share one root cause: the model's world contains a weekly cycle and no calendar events. That is the current known boundary. Spread all 79 days out and the share of misses is plain to see:

FIG.08: Error distribution across the 79 backtest days. 37 days come in under 5%, half the days under 5.7%; only 4 days exceed 20%, three of them around Memorial Day and one a small-base Saturday.

Beyond a single number, the system also gives an interval and claims eighty percent confidence that the actual lands inside it. The interval is built just as plainly: collect the backtest's daily actual-to-forecast ratios and take the 10th and 90th percentiles as the lower and upper multipliers. No distributional assumption; history says how wide the interval should be. Here is how that interval held up day by day in the backtest:

FIG.09: The 80% interval, day by day. Of the 74 evaluable days, 60 landed inside, an 81% hit rate, matching the claimed eighty percent; the orange dots are the 14 days that fell outside.

The benefit of building it this way is that the phrase "eighty percent confident" becomes checkable. And eighty is not the only rung: the same recipe produces intervals from fifty to ninety percent, and each rung's claimed confidence can be plotted against the share it actually delivered:

FIG.10: The interval calibration curve. The dashed diagonal is "said and done."

All five points hug the diagonal, meaning the interval neither bluffs nor sandbags. For the person using it, planning buffers around "four days out of five land in this range" holds up.

The design decisions that mattered

Looking back, the decisions that actually mattered in this module are mostly not in the model.

People can override the system at any time. The forecast only fills the target when it is blank; the moment a manager enters their own number, the system's steps aside. The system proposes, the person decides.

Where the number came from is always stated in the open. The page labels whether the current target was entered by a person or computed by the system, and if computed, whether the platform estimate was used and why not if it wasn't. If the system silently switched algorithms when data went missing, with no trace on the page, the end state is a number nobody can explain.

When samples are thin, it does not bluff. Under 10 days of history, no forecast. Under 30 days, the forecast ships but the page flags how few days back it. When the backtest sample is too small, there is no interval at all, stated outright, rather than a made-up default multiplier standing in.

Data gets checked before it enters the model. On days when collection broke, the database only recorded part of the day, and letting that into history would drag the whole baseline down; so any day whose recorded volume is under half the platform estimate is marked incomplete and excluded. Separately, "today" is defined by the wall clock, not by the latest date in the database; otherwise a collection outage freezes the forecast page on the day before the gap, with nothing visibly wrong.

The whole module is 400 lines, and the model is 30 of them. Everything else does the work above. Most of a forecasting system's credibility is produced outside the model.

Why not a bigger model

In 2026 this is practically a mandatory question: why not a large model, or at the very least a proper ML pipeline? The answer has four layers, and none of them is about sophistication. All of them are about the structure of the problem.

First, sample size. There are 109 days of usable history, which after day-of-week grouping is about 15 samples per group. A neural network at this scale learns to mistake historical accidents for patterns. Prophet-class time series tools earn their keep on multiple seasonalities and trend breaks, but here the weekly cycle is the only strong signal, and EWMA already wrings it dry.

Second, what remains in the error. The 7.5% still contains calendar events the model does not see. Strip those out and what's left comes from weather, upstream re-routing, and equipment failures, the day's own accidents that no model removes. A more complex model has little accuracy to win here, while its full costs arrive intact: new dependencies, a training pipeline, and failures whose causes cannot be traced.

Third, explainability, which here is a business requirement rather than a preference. Managers ask why today's number is what it is, and today the answer is one sentence: the platform said X, recent Tuesdays have run Y above it, so it's X plus Y. Swap in a black box and that sentence is gone, and a number that cannot be explained does not actually get used on the floor.

Fourth, deployment cost, which is zero. Pure standard library, running inside an existing service process, milliseconds per forecast, no model file, no training job.

Conclusion

The same year I did this project, nearly every industry conversation about forecasting orbited large models, as if a solution without a GPU did not deserve serious attention. Meanwhile this warehouse's problem was solved to within half an hour of finish time by 30 lines of weighted averages, with every step checkable and explainable, in daily use on a real floor.

This is not a claim that large models don't work. It is a claim that tools have to match problems. With 109 days of history, one overwhelming weekly cycle, and an external estimate already in hand, the work the problem actually demands is cleaning the data, using the cycle fully, and calibrating the outside information before trusting it, and a weighted average finishes all three. A bigger model has no increment to win here and pays its full costs anyway. Conversely, once a year of history accumulates and calendar events become the main contradiction, a stronger model earns its audition, and even then it has to beat these 30 lines in a backtest before it takes the job.

The toolbox is deeper than it has ever been, but judgment has not gotten any cheaper. Seeing the structure of a problem, picking a tool that is exactly enough, and spending the saved effort on data and validation is not outdated in 2026. It is scarce. Simple methods have never needed to defend themselves. They only need someone to take them seriously.
